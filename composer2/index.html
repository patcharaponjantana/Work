What retargeting is

Retargeting means taking animation that was authored or captured on one skeleton and playing it on another skeleton whose proportions, joint count, or naming differs. Game teams reuse mocap, locomotion libraries, and cinematic clips across heroes, NPCs, and creatures by retargeting instead of re-keying everything per mesh.

Pipelines still differ per engine, but the problems repeat: alignment of reference poses, matching logical joints, deciding how much translation to carry over, and fixing contacts (especially feet) after the approximation.

Unreal Engine exposes retarget chains and IK rigs; Unity uses Humanoids and Avatar mappings with muscle limits — different UI, same underlying questions this tutorial walks through.

Bind pose and hierarchy

Each skeletal mesh stores vertices weighted to joints. The bind (reference) pose is the neutral pose used when binding skin weights — often a T-pose or an engine-specific relaxed stance. Animation clips store transforms relative to that convention.

Retargeting compares logical rotations along parent chains: pelvis → spine → chest → neck → head, or shoulder → elbow → wrist. If source and target bind poses disagree (arms down vs T-pose), rotations from motion capture may aim the wrong way until you apply an offset pose or convert through a shared reference.

Takeaway: stabilize bind poses early. Alignment reduces mysterious 90° twists and makes bone maps trustworthy before you tune IK.

Bone mapping

A bone map pairs joints on the source skeleton with joints on the target skeleton. Engines may infer maps from naming patterns, but artists usually verify shoulders, elbows, wrists, hips, knees, ankles, spine levels, and neck.

High-end rigs add twist bones or helper joints for cloth and deformation. Those may need explicit mappings, derivation rules (copy percentage from parent), or be omitted if the target rig is simpler — omitting can flatten shoulders unless compensated elsewhere.

Scale and translations

Motion capture often includes joint translations in addition to rotations. When the target skeleton is taller or shorter, copying translations verbatim stretches limbs or collapses them; feet miss the ground or bury into it. Many pipelines favor rotation-driven retargeting relative to bind pose and selectively retarget translations for props or root motion only.

Skeleton vs actor scale also interacts with physics capsules and attachment sockets — always sanity-check scale before blaming the retarget solver.

Spine distribution

A common source–target mismatch is spine bone count: a source rig might have 3 spine joints (lumbar, thoracic, cervical) while the target has 5. Naively copying each rotation 1:1 leaves 2 bones with no data; the correct approach is to distribute the total arc evenly across however many bones the target has.

The same principle applies in reverse: compressing a 5-bone spine into 3 target bones sums adjacent rotations. The total arc is preserved; only the per-joint granularity changes.

Root motion vs in-place cycles

Root motion moves the character through the world using translation extracted from the animation — commonly from the pelvis or a dedicated root bone — instead of sliding an in-place cycle with gameplay code driving velocity.

Retargeting root motion requires agreeing which bone carries trajectory, whether to compensate for hip bob, and how to align cycle phases so feet don't fight the authored displacement. In-place loops stay planted by stripping horizontal translation and looping cleanly for blend trees.

IK vs FK highlights

Forward kinematics propagate rotations down chains; inverse kinematics solve joint angles to reach targets (hands on props, feet on slopes). Retargeting rarely preserves perfect foot plants — slight pelvis drift or proportion error introduces skate.

Foot IK post-processes adjust ankles/knees to keep toes planted within tolerance. Full-body IK can stabilize grabs during aiming. Each solve adds tuning cost and can conflict with strong hip translation; teams balance IK weight per locomotion state.

Takeaway: treat IK as polish on top of a sane rotation retarget, not a substitute for a wrong bone map.

Pitfalls checklist

  • Mismatched bind poses between source clips and target rig.
  • Missing bones on the target (fingers, spine splits, facial rigs) silencing channels.
  • Extreme twists near gimbal regions magnifying small quaternion differences across engines or sampling rates.
  • Over-mapping twist joints onto unrelated bones, producing corkscrew elbows until constraints clamp motion.
  • Ignoring scale factors on imported meshes versus skeleton definitions.
  • Relying solely on translations from performance capture onto cartoon proportions.

Recap and quiz

Stable bind poses and explicit bone maps translate logical motion between rigs. Rotation-centric retargeting survives proportion differences better than naive translation copy; root motion policies define how characters travel; foot IK cleans residual sliding after approximations.

Glossary

Bind / reference pose

Neutral skeleton orientation used when skinning a mesh; animation data is interpreted relative to this baseline.

Bone map

Table pairing source joints to target joints so animation channels have destinations.

Retargeting

Transferring motion authored on skeleton A onto skeleton B via pose alignment and mapping rules.

Root motion

Translation embedded in animation that moves the actor through world space instead of staying in-place.

Twist bone

Helper joint splitting twist across a limb to preserve volume during deformation.

Two-bone IK

Inverse kinematics solve for a two-joint chain (hip–knee–ankle or shoulder–elbow–wrist) using the law of cosines to position the end effector at a target location.

Spine distribution

Technique that spreads a total spine arc evenly across N target joints when the source and target have different spine bone counts.
